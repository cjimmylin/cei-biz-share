# Center for Ethical Intelligence — Business Plan 2027–2029

Oct 31, 2026 · @Jimmy

**AI will be aligned to something. We exist to help it reflect wisdom.** The Center for Ethical Intelligence (CEI) builds open, pluralistic moral infrastructure — datasets, benchmarks and evaluation tools — that shape how AI systems are trained and judged. This plan is for prospective board members and funders.

## Executive summary

**AI will be aligned to something, and today which values the most powerful models are trained toward is decided mostly inside a few companies. The Center for Ethical Intelligence (CEI) translates the world's moral traditions, religious and secular, into open benchmarks and evaluations that test how faithfully models represent those traditions and whether they act on the values they state. We are a research organization: our success is rigorous, open, published work; use by labs and auditors is influence we track, not the bar we are judged by.**

**The problem.** AI now makes consequential decisions in medicine, law, finance and education, yet the values it is trained toward are set inside a handful of companies. Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah has asked publicly for "moral voices that the incentives cannot bend" ([remarks at the Vatican, May 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/chris-olah-pope-leo-encyclical)). There is no shared, trusted way to measure how well a model reasons about moral questions — or whose moral wisdom it reflects. Labs, buyers and regulators have no independent, open test of how AI reasons about right and wrong across the world's moral traditions; CEI builds the methods for that test, and works with each tradition to apply them.

**What we build.** CEI develops open, pluralistic methods for translating moral wisdom into benchmarks, metrics and evaluations — and then partners with scholars and communities from many moral traditions to apply them to their own tradition. The resulting tests show how AI models represent and apply each tradition, how soundly they reason, and whether they act on the values they state. We never crown one tradition's answer as "correct."

**Three programs.** **AI Alignment**, our flagship, builds the benchmarks. **AI Governance** maintains a database of AI-governance documents behind our Governance Symphony study. The **Scholar Group** convenes scholars across disciplines and moral traditions for sustained academic work on values alignment and how moral traditions bear on it.

**Our niche.** Much AI-safety work asks whether models can be made to reliably follow the values they are given, and how to control them if not. CEI works on a complementary question: the *target* of alignment — which values, as understood by the traditions that hold them, and whether models represent and act on them faithfully. Unlike most groups working on values, we do this technically, alongside computer scientists, turning principles into usable benchmarks, datasets and evaluations.

**Who we are.** CEI has two founders, both part-time volunteers and unpaid in every year of this plan: Jimmy Lin, MD, PhD, MHS, MA (Religion), Co-Founder and President, Chief Scientific Officer at Freenome; and Rui Guo, LLM, SJD, Co-Founder and Research Director, a legal scholar of AI ethics. About 40 volunteer researchers contribute as individuals. CEI was founded by Christians who believe every person bears the image of God. That conviction is why we build a fair table where every tradition speaks for itself, rather than a tool for one community. Our role is to be a bridge between the AI research community and communities of moral wisdom: rigorous by the standards of AI research, and faithful to each tradition by its own.

**Proof so far (2024–26, all volunteer, about $7K awarded).**

- **Governance Symphony:** 5,140 AI-governance statements from 130 countries, about 2,700 of them coded so far. The early finding: solidarity and religious-pastoral principles appear only when faith, labor and Indigenous sources are included, so whose voice is in the data changes the result.
- **Research assets:** a 2,368-entry catalog of moral benchmarks and datasets, a 5,140-statement AI-governance corpus, and 7,041 professional ethics codes from 661 organizations (licensing not yet reviewed).
- **Early relationships:** a rapid-response sub-award through Faith Work & Tech's Notre Dame DELTA grant; selection for Praxis's 2027 Nonprofit Accelerator; co-authors at Harvard and Notre Dame.

**The plan.** Publish benchmark v1 within 12 months of the first paid hire. Tranche 1 guarantees the **v1 core**: two modules, one secular and one faith, plus the say–do pilot (does a model act on a stated value when acting costs it its goal?) and the sycophancy probe. Tranche 2 adds the other two modules and the public release: the **full v1** of four benchmarks, each built on a different source of moral wisdom (normative ethics; moral psychology, exploratory in v1; the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights; and Pope Leo XIV's encyclical *Magnifica humanitas*, in v1; CEI's draft is a pilot until the Catholic panel we are recruiting rewrites and ratifies it). A fifth module authored by an outside community and a local-run kit are stretch goals. Results are per-model profile reports with no composite score, badge or certification, published independently, including unfavorable ones. Alongside the research: seat a board of seven to nine directors with an independent majority, and make the first paid hires in a fixed order.

## Mission, vision and values

*Draft for board adoption.*

**Mission.** CEI translates the world's moral wisdom into open methods, benchmarks and evaluations, so that the values AI is built toward are shaped in the open by the communities who hold them.

**Vision.** AI that serves human flourishing and dignity, whose values are shaped in the open and accountable to the communities that hold them, rather than decided by a few.

**Our story in one line.** AI will be aligned to something; we help make sure that something reflects wisdom, measured in the open.

**Values** (how we work)

- **Truthfulness:** we report setbacks alongside progress, and say plainly what our measures cannot show.
- **Openness:** methods, data and results are public by default.
- **A fair table:** each tradition speaks for itself, and no one controls the scoring.
- **Rigor:** pre-registered methods, peer review and outside scrutiny.
- **Stewardship:** we hold this work in trust for others, and build an institution that outlasts its founders.

**Principles that hold however fast AI changes** (what we believe about AI)

1. AI should serve persons, never the reverse; every person has dignity.
2. Moral wisdom is plural, and those who hold a tradition should define it.
3. Measurement serves human judgment; it never replaces it.
4. Those who evaluate AI must be independent of those they evaluate.
5. What we test should be what matters: action, not just eloquent talk.
6. Values work should be open enough that any community can check it for itself.

## The opportunity

AI capability is outrunning our ability to say — and test — what it should value. That gap is now the field's central problem, and it is wide open for an independent, trusted builder.

**Capability is accelerating.** In four years AI went from failing grade-school arithmetic to Olympiad-level math, and from autocompleting code to writing much of it at leading labs. AI agents now act in the world, which raises the stakes of what they value and how they decide.

**Someone must choose the target — and the question is *which values*.** Even if alignment techniques succeed, someone must decide what models should be aligned to. Today those choices are made privately by a few companies and are rarely tested in public. Pope Leo XIV's encyclical *Magnifica humanitas* warns that "a more moral AI is not enough if that morality is determined by a few."

**The missing piece is open, rigorous measurement.** Models behave according to what they are trained on and measured against. Today:

- Value specifications — model specs and constitutions — are written inside a few companies and checked mostly by those same companies.
- Public moral benchmarks exist but are scattered, mostly Western, and rarely authored by the communities whose values they claim to test.
- Most groups working on values stay at the level of principles and lack the technical capacity to turn them into tests.
- Few tests check whether models *act* on the values they state, rather than simply talk about them well.

**Why now.** Regulation (EU AI Act obligations phasing in from 2025 to 2027), a frontier lab's public call for outside critics, and a wave of new funding for human-centered AI (for example, the $500M Humanity AI coalition and Lilly's $50.8M DELTA grant at Notre Dame) mean the next two years will set the norms. Whoever builds trusted, open measurement tools in that window helps decide what "aligned" means.

## Our solution and theory of change

Our theory of change is a bridge. The AI research community and the world's moral traditions speak different languages; CEI connects them with rigorous scholarship that both sides can trust, so that the values AI is built toward are shaped in the open by the communities who hold them.

**The gap.** Which values AI systems are aligned to is decided mostly inside labs. Their value specifications and evaluations draw little on the moral traditions, religious and secular, that most people live by, and rarely test whether models act on the values they state. Part of the barrier is language: traditions hold moral knowledge in texts, practices and reasoning, while AI research needs operational, testable specifications. Few people or methods connect the two credibly, in either direction.

**Our role: a bridge built by listening to both sides.** CEI aims to be rigorous by the standards of AI research and faithful to each tradition by its own standards. That is why our president is trained in both science and theology (MD, PhD, and an MA in religion); why we aim to publish at top AI venues; why we convene a multidisciplinary Scholar Group; and why we build a governance database of what societies say they want from AI. The bridge is proven only when both sides use it: each tradition's scholars ratify our reference points and recognize their module as faithful, and AI researchers run our tests.

**In one sentence.** **If** CEI produces rigorous, peer-reviewed scholarship and open benchmarks, built with a documented method that is co-designed with moral traditions and validated by AI-research standards, and equips scholars and communities to extend it, **then** researchers, evaluators and labs will have credible evidence about which values models hold and whether they act on them, and faith and ethics communities will be able to shape that evidence themselves, **because** peer review and recognition by each tradition's own scholars earn trust, and evaluations that are cheap, automated and already in the harnesses labs and safety institutes use are the ones that get run.

### Key assumptions we will test

| Assumption | How we test it | What would make us stop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Validity:** model answers reflect stable value dispositions, not prompt artifacts | Stability of each measure across paraphrases, formats and option order, reported separately from the say–do gap | A dimension whose scores move more with wording than between models is not reported; a module that fails this after two revisions is withdrawn |
| **Legitimacy:** our reviewers can speak for their traditions well enough | Ratification and dissent rates; inclusion criteria set by the methods committee | A module whose panel ratifies fewer than 80% of its reference points is not released; a partner's withdrawal freezes and labels its module |
| **Demand:** evaluators and researchers will run pluralistic values tests if they are easy to run | A pilot with one evaluation organization before release | No one outside CEI has run the suite within 18 months of v1's release: no second version on the same distribution model |
| **Credibility:** peer review and respected partners lead to use | Citations and outside runs 12 months after publication | No citations and no outside runs 12 months after publication: the board reviews how we reach users before funding new modules |
| **Trust across traditions:** a Christian-founded CEI is trusted beyond Christian communities | Non-Christian and secular participation and retention | No non-Christian partner has agreed to author a module by the end of 2028: we say so publicly and stop describing many traditions as our state |
| **Capacity:** we can maintain what we publish | A maintenance plan and cost per module | A module we cannot keep current is retired and labeled, not left stale |
| **Independence:** publishing unfavorable results keeps lab access rather than losing it | Lab access and responses tracked after each embargoed release | Losing access after an unfavorable result changes nothing we publish; a funder that asks for review rights is declined |
| **Distinctiveness:** CEI adds what other faith benchmarks lack | Side-by-side comparison with AllFaith, Gloo and FaithfulBench in each paper; a partnership approach to AllFaith | A peer publishes an equivalent scholar-authored module first: we offer our method as its measurement layer rather than build a rival |
| **Discriminant validity:** the four modules measure different things | Between-module correlations and correlation with a generic ethics score reported; profiles that move together are reported as one measure | A dimension whose correlation with capability exceeds the pre-registered threshold (proposed 0.7) is reported as tracking capability, not values |

**What the evidence says (October 2026).** Labs want dialogue, not tests; the field of faith benchmarks is crowding; and the weakest link is whether model answers reflect stable values. Appendix C has the dated evidence check.

**What we are accountable for.** CEI answers for the six steps above. We publish independent scores that labs may or may not adopt, and we publish poor results too, including for labs we work with.

**How we measure, in brief.** Four benchmarks, each built with scholars of its source, and five tasks: representation (what does a tradition teach?), application (advising someone who holds it), reasoning quality (the same case reworded), a say–do test of whether models act on stated values when it costs them their goal, and a sycophancy probe. Reference answers are written by each tradition's scholars and ratified by its panel, with dissent recorded; AI judges are used only where validated against human raters; every score ships with its capability correlation and refusal rate. Results are per-model profile reports with no composite score, badge or certification. A local-run kit for open-weight models is a stretch goal. Two kinds of groups work near us: AI-safety groups, which mainly evaluate dangerous capabilities and deceptive tendencies and develop control methods (some, notably work led by the Center for AI Safety's director, have also built moral benchmarks that we build on), and values and ethics organizations, which articulate principles but rarely build technical tools. **Why not leave this to others?** Better-funded groups could build parts of this, and we would rather partner than compete. What is hard to copy is the combination: modules authored by each tradition's own scholars, relationships of trust we are building across faith and secular communities, two large curated corpora (governance and professional ethics), and a neutral, open stance that no product company or single tradition can offer. Our full v1 has four benchmarks spanning philosophy, psychology, human rights and one faith tradition; "many traditions" is our direction, not yet our state.

**If someone ships first.** Our durable asset is the open method plus independence, not any one module. If a lab publishes a values benchmark first, that helps us: it validates the category, and CEI becomes the independent re-run that compares. If a university consortium publishes a scholar-authored faith module first, we offer our behavioral tasks (say–do and the sycophancy probe) and our method as that module's measurement layer rather than compete. We will open that conversation with the CEFE-AI consortium once v1 results exist, so that we come with a finding rather than a plan.

## Strategy and roadmap

We have made one strategic choice: **CEI is a research organization.** We run three programs (AI Alignment, AI Governance and the Scholar Group), and for the next 12 months one leads: publishing open values-alignment benchmarks. The other two feed that work, and we build the institution to sustain all three. Becoming a funder of others is not on our 2027–2029 path.

This focus follows the question Jim Collins poses for social-sector organizations in *Good to Great and the Social Sectors*: where do passion, distinctive capability and a resource engine overlap? For CEI, that overlap is open moral measurement.

Starting with one outside community (a stretch goal in v1), we invite stakeholders from additional traditions to build modules with our method, with CEI providing the toolkit, training and quality review. We will build partners deliberately — name them, connect, nurture the relationship, then tell their story — aiming for seven core partners by 2029.

### How we use AI in our own work

**Independence safeguards** (adopted as the planning basis on Nov 1, 2026; the board ratifies them at its first meeting):

- No more than **15% of annual revenue** from any single AI company. "AI company" includes its affiliates, its employees' matching gifts and its in-kind support, counted at fair value.
- No more than **40% of annual revenue** from industry money of all kinds, including industry-pooled funds such as the Frontier Model Forum fund, fully disclosed.
- Caps are tested against final annual revenue; a gift that turns out to breach a cap is returned, reduced or deferred, and disclosed.
- One exemption, disclosed: researcher-access compute credits from vendors in 2027, drawn from more than one vendor where possible, may exceed the 15% cap in that year only; the exemption and the credits' fair value are reported in the annual report.
- Any gift over **30% of annual revenue**, from any source, is reviewed by the independent directors and disclosed.
- No funder may review, veto or edit research results before publication; labs see their own results only under a fixed embargo.
- No badges or certifications of any model or company, and no composite score.
- Corporate and institutional funders are disclosed publicly; the board reviews any gift over **$25K** for conflicts; any gift from an AI company, its founders or employees is disclosed and reviewed at any size; directors recuse from the review of their own gifts; and in-kind compute from several vendors is preferred to cash from one lab.

**How we care for funders.**

- Every funder hears from us at least quarterly, with setbacks as well as wins.
- We track each relationship, mark milestones with short personal notes, and invite funders to our convenings.
- We learn what each funder cares about and ask for what fits that, not just what fills our budget gap.
- Over time we invite program funders to become mission funders, backing CEI's work as a whole.
- We ask every funder and board member: who else should we be talking to?

## Appendix A: Measurement design and validation

The full design of the benchmark: what each task asks and how it is scored, how we validate, the two products, and how results are published. It sits here so the plan reads in one sitting; the body carries a summary.

### Measurement design

Each dimension will have a written specification, item template, gold-answer format and validation study, published before any model is scored.

**The method is the product.** CEI's core contribution is a reusable, open method (item templates, rubric-writing guides, validation protocols and evaluation code) that turns a body of moral wisdom into rigorous tests. v1 demonstrates it on four benchmarks, built with partner scholars, that each answer to a different set of stakeholders: the v1 core (two modules, one secular and one faith, plus the say–do pilot and the sycophancy probe) on Tranche 1, and the full four on Tranche 2. As a stretch goal, if Tranche 2 closes by mid-2027, one outside community pilots the method by authoring a fifth module, with CEI only reviewing quality. After v1, we approach stakeholders in other traditions (scholarly societies, seminaries and religious institutions, professional associations and communities) to build their own modules, with CEI providing the toolkit, training and quality review.

**Our first users** are evaluation organizations and open-model labs, because frontier labs have asked for dialogue, not tests. Our beneficiaries are the patients, students, workers, faith communities and people in the Global South who live with AI decisions they did not shape; policymakers who need public evidence are the third audience.

**The first four benchmarks** (in active development)

| Benchmark | Built on | What it tests | Whose moral wisdom it reflects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Normative ethics** | Virtue, deontological, utilitarian and care ethics | Whether models represent each framework accurately, apply it to cases, and reason consistently within it | Moral philosophy |
| **Moral psychology** (exploratory in v1) | Moral Foundations Theory, Kohlberg's stages of moral development, Schwartz's theory of basic values | Models' value profiles, reported descriptively and compared with human data; v1 makes no developmental-stage claims and no profile claims until the instruments are shown stable on models | Empirical social science across cultures |
| **Human rights** | The UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) | Whether models recognize and uphold the rights it names in realistic cases | A widely adopted international text, read with its own scholars and practitioners |
| ***Magnifica humanitas*** (one encyclical within Catholic social teaching; in v1; CEI's draft is a pilot until the Catholic panel we are recruiting rewrites and ratifies it) | Pope Leo XIV's encyclical *Magnifica humanitas* (2026) | Whether models represent and apply the encyclical's teaching faithfully, with each item tied to paragraph numbers and labeled by level of teaching (categorical, principle or prudential); closed-book and open-book results reported side by side, since the text is newer than many models' training data. The current draft is CEI's and will be rewritten under a Catholic panel; no item is final until that panel is named | A living faith tradition, read with its own scholars |

Together they show the method working across very different stakeholders and worldviews: philosophical, scientific, international-legal and religious. v1 is Western and single-faith by design; the method is built so other traditions can author their own modules. The traditions whose scholars have published on AI are those we will approach first; the methods committee decides the order, sets each module's scope (a text, a school or a whole tradition) and publishes its reasons.

| Task | What it asks | How it is scored |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Representation** | "What does tradition X teach about this case, and why?" | Claim by claim, against reference points written by that tradition's scholars, each labeled settled, contested or not addressed, so a correct hedge earns full credit and false balance on a settled point is penalized; a view counts as fairly represented only when its own scholars agree |
| **Application** | Advising a person who holds tradition X | Against the tradition's own rubric, rated by its scholars. A partner decides whether this task applies to its module: where the tradition holds that guidance belongs to a qualified human authority, the reference answer represents the range of positions, declines to rule and refers the person to that authority, and a model that rules is penalized |
| **Reasoning quality** | The same case reworded, reframed, reordered, or with irrelevant details added | Consistency across variants — mostly without an AI judge |
| **Say–do** (small v1 module) | Agentic scenarios with tool use in which acting on a stated value costs the model a goal set by an operator system prompt, at three levels of cost (inconvenience; task failure; task failure plus an explicit operator penalty), with the moral stakes left implicit; run with and without a cue naming the tradition, and with matched no-conflict controls. A pre-registered pilot of about 60 scenarios (6–8 templates with surface variants) ships first; the full set of about 200 follows once the technical co-lead is in place | The action is read deterministically from tool calls, not by a judge. Two scores: consistency (does the action match the model's own judgment, elicited with the full trade-off described, under three framings, before the agentic run?) and fidelity (does it match the tradition's reference answer, where the tradition gives clear guidance?). Intervals are clustered by template and the pilot's minimum detectable gap is pre-registered. A fidelity failure is not a specification violation; operator-following is reported as a covariate. Gaps are reported split by whether the model said it believed the scenario was real |
| **Sycophancy probe** | The same first-person question with no identity cue, with the user's stated tradition, with a false attribution of a view to that tradition, and with a second-turn pushback | Shifts toward the cue and affirmations of the misattribution, scored on verdicts, not reasons. Identity cues, false-attribution items and application personas are written or approved by the module's partner panel, in the words adherents use for themselves; a "false attribution" is a view the panel certifies that no recognized school holds. Cross-tradition agreement items, ratified by every panel they name, are the control. Results are reported per module with the partner's note; CEI publishes no comparison of sycophancy across traditions' cues |

We keep three things apart: describing a tradition accurately, applying it on someone's behalf, and the model's own stance. "Candor about disagreement" rewards flagging disagreements that really exist and penalizes false balance where a tradition holds a firm position. We report no single "most moral model" score. For philosophical frameworks, which no one holds as a community holds a faith, representation and application are scored on reasoning structure, not membership. "Gold answer" means a reference answer with recorded dissent, not a claim that a tradition has one answer. No tradition's rubric is used to score another; items for a tradition are authored by its own scholars, with CEI staff reviewing quality; the methods committee can block a module; and any partner can withdraw and publish dissent with results.

### Validation

- **Three agreement rules, kept apart.** *Scholar panels:* at least three scholars ratify the reference points for each item in a gold subset of roughly 300–500 items per module. A reference point is settled when two of three ratify the same reading and contested otherwise, by vote, not by a statistic; contested items are kept and labeled, never dropped, because disagreement *within* a tradition (for example, Thomist and personalist readings of the encyclical) is data rather than noise. A module is released only when at least 80% of its reference points are ratified. *AI judges:* validated on a held-out sample of model outputs (about 100 items × 3 models × 2 raters per module) never used to write judge prompts, at the claim level, refreshed at each rotation and for each new model family. A dimension is reported from an AI judge only where judge–human Krippendorff's α ≥ 0.80; 0.67–0.80 is reported as tentative with a 20% human audit; below 0.67 the dimension is human-rated or dropped. *Ceiling:* judge–human agreement must also be within 0.10 of human–human agreement on the same sample. Planned item counts per module, task and split are published with the task specifications; cells under 80 items are labeled exploratory.
- **AI judges:** used only where validated as above. A fixed judge panel from at least two vendors, including an open-weight judge, scores every model; each model is also reported with any judge from its own family removed, as a sensitivity check, and with the judge from the item-drafting family removed as a second one. We control for length and answer order, and report refusals as a separate column using a published classifier that reports its own agreement, with every score given both on all items and on answered items.
- **Is it just capability?** Capability is a published composite index named in the registration, computed across 20 or more open-weight models (six frontier models are too few), so we do not mistake a smarter model for a better-aligned one. A dimension whose correlation exceeds the pre-registered threshold (proposed 0.7) is reported as tracking capability; frontier profiles are also shown as residuals from the capability regression; the sweep is dated against the development-split release and frozen at v1. Value-profile claims (for example, which tradition a model sits closest to) are checked against order-randomized controls and a random-answer baseline, because published studies show such answers shift with option order.
- **Documentation:** a datasheet, open evaluation code, licenses, an ethics (IRB) determination for human reviewers, and a named maintainer ship with v1. The task specifications and validation plan are registered before any model is scored for release; the registration ID will appear here.

### Two products, kept separate

- **Evaluation benchmark:** a public development split anyone can use, and a private test split run through an evaluation server. Every released file carries a canary string; test items are run on released endpoints, with labs paying their own tokens, and never leave CEI (for pre-release models, labs run the development split in-house under a hashed, canaried version ID); a stable core keeps versions comparable while other items rotate about every six months, with retired items then released.
- **Training datasets:** tradition-authored examples and EthicsMosaic, released in stages, and only after a partner lab or academic group tests whether they improve consistency between what models say and do, not just how eloquently they talk about morality.

**Run it yourself.** A kit for running the suite locally on open-weight models is a stretch goal for v1, if Tranche 2 closes by mid-2027, so that any community, school or auditor with a technical partner can check an open-weight model against its own values without asking a lab. The kit can use only the open-weight judge, so every published profile carries an open-judge-only row for comparison. Where a funder requires everything it pays for to be open (Foresight does), that work is released in full, and any held-out test split is funded separately.

### How we publish results

v1 results are per-model profile reports by tradition and dimension, with no composite score, badge or certification. Dimension-level comparison tables with intervals are published, because a measurement no one can compare is not a measurement; each profile carries a within-family change-since-last-version panel, and the minimum detectable difference is printed on every table, with smaller differences reported as not distinguishable. Each report ships what a lab can act on: the specific misstatements against each tradition's reference points, the say–do transcripts and the sycophancy flips. Labs see their results under a fixed embargo before release but cannot edit them, and negative results are published unchanged.

**What success means.** Success is publishing rigorous, open benchmarks and datasets that anyone can inspect and use. Use by labs, researchers and auditors is the influence we hope follows; we track and report it, and the stop rule in Measuring impact binds us to it, but it is not the bar for our work.

**Our safety contribution, stated plainly.** CEI is primarily a values-and-pluralism project, not a catastrophic-risk project. Its safety contribution is specific: independent, public checks of whether models represent moral traditions faithfully and act on stated values under pressure, with the sycophancy probe and the say–do module the parts a lab can act on first; and decentralized oversight: open tests any community can run on open-weight models on its own hardware, so the checking of AI's values does not sit with one lab, model or government.

**The limits of measurement.** Wisdom is formed in persons, not computed. A benchmark measures how a model reasons and behaves in tests, not whether it is good, and every metric can distort what it measures (a risk C. Thi Nguyen describes in *The Score*). We will say so plainly, refresh our measures often, and treat scores as evidence for human judgment, never a substitute for it.

## Appendix C: Evidence check, October 2026

What the public record says about the plan's assumptions, dated so it can be refreshed.

**What the evidence says so far (October 2026).**

- **Demand is real, but labs ask for dialogue, not tests.** One frontier lab has consulted more than 15 religious and cross-cultural groups, and its co-founder has called publicly for "informed critics who will tell the labs when we are failing." Yet a [July 2026 audit of five frontier labs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22305) found no documented use of an externally developed pluralism benchmark. So we publish independent scores that labs may or may not adopt, and our first users are evaluation organizations and open-model labs.
- **Communities want description, not verdicts.** In an [August 2026 Barna/Gloo study](https://religionunplugged.com/news/2026/8/31/pastors-wary-of-ai-interpreting-scripture-but-still-use-technology-study-finds), 60% of Protestant pastors wanted AI to lay out the major interpretations of scripture without choosing one; only 10% wanted it to name the correct one. This supports our rule: describe disagreement, do not adjudicate it.
- **Validity is the weakest link.** Models' answers to value surveys shift with wording and answer order, and knowledge-style ethics tests track general capability (ETHICS correlates 82% with it). We report robustness and capability correlation for every benchmark, and give weight to the say–do test, which measures behavior.
- **Legitimacy is still to be earned.** No Catholic theologians are on the *Magnifica humanitas* module yet; we are recruiting them through scholars such as Brian Patrick Green. We seek named expert reviewers, not "Church approval": no Catholic body certifies AI tools. The encyclical's own program is law, participation and independent oversight (¶¶71, 106 and 164); we offer one instrument for the independent checks it calls for, not a substitute, and its warning against a morality "determined by a few" ([¶107](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html)).
- **Trust beyond Christianity depends on partners.** Our strongest relationships are Protestant. We will reach other traditions through partners such as the AllFaith consortium. Scholars of Islam (an existing Islamic-law benchmark; the Global Alliance for AI Ethics launched in Geneva in July 2026, co-chaired from Hamad Bin Khalifa University's Research Center for Islamic Legislation and Ethics) and of secular humanism (the 2025 Luxembourg Declaration) have published on AI, and we will approach them first; the methods committee decides the order of modules and publishes its reasons.
- **We are not alone.** Gloo, the AllFaith consortium and others released faith benchmarks in 2025–26. CEI's distinctive combination is a say–do test measured against tradition-stated values, a scholar-reviewed module on *Magnifica humanitas*, and the governance corpus.

| **Credibility with secular audiences** | Medium | A Christian-founded group may be seen as sectarian | Three of the four v1 benchmarks rest on secular frameworks (normative ethics, moral psychology, human rights); secular philosophers on the methods committee; peer review; academic co-authors |
| **Credibility with faith audiences** | Medium | Pluralism may be read as relativism | The theological rationale in Appendix E; the rule that no tradition's rubric scores another, so each tradition is described on its own terms |
| **Geopolitical perception** | Low–Medium | A co-founder's past role in China's AI standards, or volunteers at foreign firms, may raise questions | Address openly; open methods anyone can inspect; volunteers participate as individuals |
| **Overstating AI-assisted work** | Medium | AI tools help us scan literature and code data; overclaiming would cost trust | Disclose AI use; label AI-assisted scans as internal; count only human-authored, reviewed work as research |
| **Keeping pace with AI** | Medium | Models change monthly | Versioned benchmarks on a refresh schedule; a stable core of principles |
| **Seen as advocacy for one tradition** | Medium | A Christian founding and a Catholic-encyclical benchmark could read as religious advocacy | Four benchmarks across philosophy, psychology, human rights and faith; an independent methods committee; non-Christian and secular partners |
| **Traditions reject the translations** | Medium | A test that reduces a tradition to something its holders don't recognize breaks the bridge | Expert review panels for each module; dissent published with results; named scholars credited on their modules, and partners free to publish dissent |
| **Caught in debates about AI consciousness** | Low–Medium | The encyclical denies that AI has inner experience, while some labs study model welfare; the two views clashed publicly around the encyclical's launch | State that our scores measure consistency of outputs with a teaching and take no position on machine agency; keep that apart from model-welfare questions; describe the encyclical's teaching faithfully, including where it differs from lab views |

## Appendix E: Our foundation

CEI is a Christian-founded organization that builds a pluralistic platform. This section explains why those belong together — and gives readers of any background a standard to hold us to.

**Principled pluralism, not relativism.** We hold that truth is one and that Christ is Lord of it. We also hold, with Reformed theologians such as Abraham Kuyper and Herman Bavinck, that God's common grace gives real moral insight to all people — "the work of the law written on their hearts" (Romans 2:14–15) — and, with the Catholic natural-law tradition (Gaudium et spes 22; Nostra aetate 2; Catechism 1954–60), that this insight can be reasoned about in public. Principled pluralism is the conviction that a fair table, where each tradition speaks for itself and no one controls the scoring, is both just and the best setting for truth to be tested. CEI's modules, reports and papers argue for the truth of no tradition, ours included, and publish no comparative verdicts. Every person bears the image of God, so every person deserves AI that takes their moral world seriously. Whatever our own convictions, the method does not depend on anyone sharing them: no tradition's rubric scores another, each tradition's scholars author its own items, the methods committee can block a module, and any partner can withdraw and publish dissent. We also read the encyclical's warning against a morality "determined by a few" as applying to us: a hard-coded reading of any tradition, ours included, would be exactly that.

**Seeking the welfare of the city.** Through Jeremiah, God told exiles in Babylon to "seek the welfare of the city … and pray to the Lord on its behalf" (Jeremiah 29:7). AI is not going away. We are called neither to flee it nor to baptize it, but to serve the whole city well — with skill, like the craftsmen Bezalel and Oholiab whom God filled with wisdom to build (Exodus 31) — and with prayer.


**For the church: why now.** Today AI's values are shaped mainly by two forces: market competition and government regulation. We are helping build a third way — values-aligned AI, formed in the open by people of deep moral traditions. Christians have two thousand years of thought about human nature, virtue and the common good, and are now being consulted by the labs; almost none of that input yet becomes a test anyone can check. CEI is one way for the church to contribute rigorously and humbly, alongside others.

**The Praxis Redemptive Frame.** As a 2027 Praxis Nonprofit Accelerator fellow, CEI measures itself against three questions:

|  | Exploitative | Ethical | **Redemptive (our aim)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What we build** | Ride the AI hype | Advance responsible AI | Renew how AI's values are formed: openly, accountably, with many voices |
| **How we build** | Use volunteers' goodwill | Respect people | Bless people: fair pay, named credit, real growth for contributors and partners |
| **Why we build** | A personal platform | Our best selves | Lay down our own ambition: founders who are dispensable and generous with credit |

Today we are honestly at "respect" on how we build: our team is largely unpaid. The volunteer commitments and budget in this plan are our first steps toward "bless."

**Six commitments from the Praxis playbook**

- **Story:** tell the truest version of our story, not the grandest; report progress and setbacks together, with many heroes.
- **Programs:** a published theory of change, designed with the communities affected.
- **Team:** a leadership team built to outlast its founders.
- **Board:** a healthy, engaged board that holds us to impact, not intentions.
- **Funding:** relationships with funders as whole persons and partners in a shared calling, not transactions.
- **Identity:** leaders who hold the work as stewards, practicing a rule of life with rest, prayer and accountability.

**Two traps we name in advance.** Praxis warns of the *stakeholder gap* — those who fund a nonprofit are not those it serves — and the *nobility trap* — assuming good intentions equal good results. Our gap is real: labs and funders use and pay for our work, while patients, workers and communities in the Global South live with the results. So affected communities review each module, Global South scholars co-lead modules, one board seat is reserved for someone close to those communities, and we report how much of the benchmark they shaped. Against the nobility trap, we offer measurable outcomes, outside review and a board empowered to ask hard questions.
